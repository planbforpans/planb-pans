Field Guide · After the Infection

Leg pain after strep.
Growing pains, or something else?

My daughter developed migratory joint pain after strep. A specialist raised acute rheumatic fever about her a year ago. My son has had intermittent leg pain that disappeared within days of starting amoxicillin. Neither of them has ever had an echocardiogram.

I am writing this because I only just put it together myself. If I missed it with two children and a folder full of labs, other people are missing it too.

The one sentence to hold onto

Growing pains do not respond to antibiotics. If your child’s leg pain goes away when they start an antibiotic, that is not a coincidence — it is information about what was causing it. Most childhood leg pain is genuinely benign. But pain that tracks with an infection, and lifts when the infection is treated, is telling you something, and it is worth a calm conversation with your doctor.

Two things worth knowing the names of

Post-streptococcal reactive arthritis

Joint pain that follows a strep infection. Usually milder, often stays in one place rather than travelling, and settles once the infection is properly treated. This is the common one, and it is not dangerous. It is also the one that best fits leg pain which quietly disappears on antibiotics.

Acute rheumatic fever

Joint pain that moves — a knee this week, an ankle the next — is its classic presentation, typically appearing two to four weeks after a strep throat. It is uncommon in the United States but it is not rare, and rates have been climbing.

Here is the part that matters: the danger of rheumatic fever is not the joints. It is the heart. It can inflame heart valves silently — no chest pain, no breathlessness, no symptoms at all — and that damage can be permanent. The joints hurt, and then stop hurting, and the heart quietly keeps the scar.

How to tell ordinary growing pains from something to ask about

Most leg pain in childhood really is benign, and nobody should be frightened by a normal eight-year-old’s achy shins. Here is how the two tend to look different.

Looks like growing pains

  • Both legs, rather than one.
  • Muscles — shins, calves, thighs — rather than joints.
  • Evening or night, and gone by morning.
  • No limp. Running and playing normally the next day.
  • No swelling, warmth or redness.
  • Rubbing it helps, and your child lets you.

Worth a call to your doctor

  • One joint, or pain that moves between joints.
  • Swelling, warmth or redness over a joint.
  • A limp, or refusing to put weight on the leg.
  • Pain still there in the morning, not just at bedtime.
  • Fever alongside the pain.
  • Your child will not let you touch it.
  • Any chest pain, breathlessness, or a racing or pounding heart.

One further clue clinicians use: rheumatic fever joint pain responds dramatically to ibuprofen — not merely a bit better, but close to switched off. If that is what you are seeing, say so. It is a meaningful detail, not a trivial one.

The one test almost nobody orders

An echocardiogram

An ultrasound of the heart. No needle, no sedation, usually under an hour, and your child can watch the screen. It is the test that finds the silent valve inflammation rheumatic fever can cause.

In our world it is very rarely ordered, because when a child has new OCD, tics or rage, everyone — reasonably — is looking at the brain. But if your child has had joint or leg pain following a strep infection, the heart is the organ where a missed answer is permanent rather than merely delayed.

And if you ask for bloodwork, ask for both tests

Rheumatic fever can only be diagnosed alongside evidence of a preceding strep infection — which means the strep antibody tests matter here, not just the throat swab.

Ask for antistreptolysin O (ASO) and anti-DNase B. Both, as two separate orders. ASO alone misses roughly one in five children, because not everyone mounts an ASO response at all. Anti-DNase B rises later and stays elevated far longer, so together they cover a much wider window than either does alone.

This is the single most common omission I see in these records. A normal ASO on its own does not rule strep out — it only means half the test was done.

Bottom line

Leg or joint pain that follows a strep infection, or that eases when an antibiotic starts, is worth taking seriously — not with panic, but with a question at a normal appointment. Most of it will be post-streptococcal reactive arthritis, or nothing at all. The reason to ask anyway is that the one serious possibility damages the heart quietly, and the test that rules it out is an ultrasound with no needle in it.

I am booking echocardiograms for both of mine. This is parent education, not medical advice — take it to your team as questions.
