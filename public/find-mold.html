What this targets

The “gold standard” that was built on almost nothing

The ERMI (Environmental Relative Moldiness Index) is sold as the gold-standard home mold test. So we read the original 2007 study that created it. Here is the honest summary — the parts no lab selling you the test will tell you:

What the ERMI study actually says

  • It was built for population research, not to diagnose your home. The authors’ own conclusion: the index “may be useful for home mold-burden estimates in epidemiological studies.”
  • In the authors’ own words: “the ERMI is a mold index, not a health index.” It was never designed to tell you whether mold is making a person sick.
  • It is a RELATIVE score. It was built from vacuumed dust in 1,096 U.S. homes in 2004. Your number just says where your house ranks against those homes — so by design, about half of all homes score “above average.” “Above average” is not the same as dangerous.
  • It’s a hypersensitive DNA (qPCR) test. It counts mold DNA, so it picks up spores tracked in from outside — through a window, on the dog, on your shoes — not just mold growing in your house.
  • The consumer version collects dust with a Swiffer cloth that ships in a cardboard box and can pick up stray spores — so the same house can score very differently depending on how and where you wipe.
  • It was never validated to tell you whether your home is making your child sick — yet it became the marketed “gold standard” anyway.

Source: Vesper et al., “Development of an Environmental Relative Moldiness Index for US Homes,” J. Occup. Environ. Med. 2007 · and the EPA’s own statement that ERMI is “a research tool… not validated for non-research purposes.”

Our advice: skip it

We don’t recommend ERMI or HERTSMI-2 dust tests. A number built for research can’t tell you where the water is, and the water is what you have to find. Spend that money on the two tools below and an independent IEP instead.

How to find it yourself

Mold follows moisture. Find the water and you’ve found the mold. Here’s the order we use.

1 · Get two tools (about $30 each)

  • A pinless moisture meter (for example Klein ET140 or General Tools MMD7NP). It reads moisture inside walls and floors without making holes. Find one
  • An infrared thermometer gun (for example Etekcity Lasergrip or Klein IR1). Damp drywall reads colder than the dry wall beside it. Find one

2 · Walk the house

Run both tools along baseboards, under windows, around tubs, showers and toilets, under sinks, behind the washer and fridge, the AC closet and air handler, and ceilings under bathrooms. You’re comparing, not hunting for a magic number: one wall much wetter or colder than the wall beside it is the finding. Also note any musty smell, stains, bubbling paint, warped floors, or a room your child does worse in. Write it all down with the room and the wall.

3 · Hire an independent IEP

An Indoor Environmental Professional (IEP) who only tests and does not sell remediation, so there’s no conflict of interest. Many states license mold assessors separately from remediators (in Texas, look for a TDLR-licensed Mold Assessment Consultant). Hand them your list, and ask them to look for hidden moisture inside walls, in the AC and ducts, and under sinks, not just to take air samples. Check their reviews and license before you book.

4 · Fix the water first, then remove the material

Nothing else matters until the moisture source is gone: the leak, the sweating duct, the grading, the gutter. Then wet drywall and insulation get cut out and thrown away, with sealed plastic containment and a HEPA air machine, and your family out of the house during the work. Be wary of fogging, ozone or “encapsulation” sold instead of removal.

5 · Only then, detox

A mold detox for your child comes after the source is found and they’re out of it, with their practitioner. Detoxing while they’re still being exposed doesn’t work, and it can make them feel worse.
