Natural treatment for PANDAS.
What has evidence, and what doesn’t.

Many parents search for natural options because the medical path feels slow, scary, or out of reach. Here is the honest version: no natural approach has been shown to replace treating the infection and the inflammation behind PANS and PANDAS. But several have real evidence for helping your child alongside that care, and knowing which is which saves time, money and hope.

The honest frame

If strep or another infection is active, treating it comes first. Natural approaches work best alongside medical care, not instead of it.

Tell your child’s doctor about every supplement. “Natural” does not mean safe in every dose or combination, and some interact with medications. We’re not doctors. Everything here is made to take to yours.

Strongest evidence: help for the symptoms themselves

Therapy for OCD and anxiety (CBT with ERP)

Cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention is the standard first treatment for childhood OCD, and the PANS Research Consortium’s published guidance recommends it for children with PANS, adapted to a child who may be in a flare. It doesn’t treat the trigger, but it gives your child tools that keep working between flares.

Protect sleep

Sleep problems are part of PANS itself, and a child who isn’t sleeping has less to fight with. A steady routine, a dark, cool room, and less screen time before bed are basic and they matter. If sleep stays broken, raise it with your doctor rather than adding supplements on your own.

A calmer household

Parent coaching that lowers “accommodation” of OCD rituals, and simple nervous-system regulation, help many families get through flares. See what to do during a meltdown and nervous-system regulation.

Some evidence: worth discussing with your doctor

Vitamin D, after a test

Small studies have found lower vitamin D levels in children with PANDAS and with OCD. Whether supplementing improves symptoms hasn’t been proven, but a low level is easy to find with a blood test and easy to correct. Test first, then supplement to the dose your doctor suggests.

Omega-3 fatty acids

Fish-oil omega-3s are anti-inflammatory and have been studied in children’s mood and anxiety, with mixed results. They have not been tested in PANS trials. Generally well tolerated; ask about dose and any bleeding risk before procedures.

Probiotics during antibiotics

Probiotics are commonly used to reduce antibiotic-associated diarrhea, which matters when a child is on long courses. Whether they change PANS symptoms is unknown. For the bigger gut picture, see gut and the microbiome in PANS.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC)

NAC has been studied as an add-on for OCD, including one small trial in children, with mixed results. It hasn’t been studied in PANS. Discuss it with your doctor before starting.

An anti-inflammatory diet

No diet has trial evidence in PANS, but many families find certain foods flare their child. See the PANS diet for how to look for triggers without over-restricting a child who may already be eating very little.

Not natural, but often the first step

Ibuprofen and other NSAIDs

Parents looking for a gentle option are often surprised that the first thing many PANS doctors try is an ordinary anti-inflammatory like ibuprofen. An observational study of children with PANS found NSAIDs were associated with shorter flares. Use only with your doctor’s dosing, because long courses carry stomach and kidney risks. Little evidence in PANS so far

Families also try homeopathy, herbal antimicrobials, brain retraining, and detox protocols. Some families report real help. None has been tested in PANS trials yet. If you try one, change one thing at a time and track your child daily, so you can see what is actually working.

Go slowly

Stacking many supplements at once makes it impossible to tell what helps and what hurts, and some herbs interact with antibiotics and other medications. Bring the full list to every appointment.

Bottom line

Treat the trigger first, then build the natural layer around it. The approaches with the best evidence are the least glamorous: therapy for OCD, sleep, and a calmer household. Supplements like vitamin D are worth discussing, ideally after a test. Everything else is worth trying carefully, one change at a time, with daily tracking so you know what’s working.

Sources: PANS Research Consortium treatment guidelines (Thienemann et al., 2017; Frankovich et al., 2017; Cooperstock et al., 2017, Journal of Child and Adolescent Psychopharmacology); Brown et al., 2017, on NSAIDs and flare duration in PANS.
